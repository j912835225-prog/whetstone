# prose

**One line:** a piece has to fit the occasion first, the facts second, and the ear last.

The spine of this checklist is language-neutral, because the way prose fails is language-neutral: written before the reader was decided, inflated to look substantial, cut by word count instead of by meaning. What differs between Chinese and English is the surface - which is why those are two short sections at the end rather than two separate skills.

Most items come from classical Chinese criticism, kept only where they can be mechanically executed: Liu Xie on register and occasion, Li Yu on cutting and word choice. Where an English convention says the same thing more usefully, it is the English one here.

## The checklist

| Moment | What to do | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Before writing | Settle three things: who reads it, on what occasion, and what they should do afterwards. If any of the three cannot be settled, do not start | The occasion sets the register and the register sets the words; without the three you are talking to yourself |
| Choosing register | Official letter, application, reply: correct, not one word extra. Narrative, lyric: clear, never cloying. Notice, instruction, correction: fact first, verdict after, nothing left ambiguous. Explanation, note, history: only what has been verified. Elegy, inscription: weight and depth, no ornament | Readers read according to the occasion. Get the register wrong and they distrust the writer before they distrust the writing |
| Outlining | Answer three questions before drafting: what should the reader feel; which few events carry that; what can go unsaid | Skip these and you write wherever the sentence leads you; the deadwood is guaranteed, and cutting costs more than writing did |
| When tired | Stop. Do not push through | The paragraph written on willpower is the one you delete the next morning |
| It feels wordy | Cut the modifiers, keep the facts. If the meaning is intact after the cut, the cut was right; if the meaning is now short, you cut too far | The test is meaning, not word count |
| Words before thought, or thought before words | Have something to say, then find the words. Manufactured feeling is visible in one paragraph | Writing to seem to have something is the fastest way to lose a reader |
| Wanting to be original | Change one thing inside an established form; do not replace the form | The form keeps it readable, the change carries the new; replace everything and the reader has no handhold |
| Wanting to be striking | Stand it up first, then take the risk | The striking comes out of the sound; striking without sound is a miss |
| Quotation, allusion | Few, exact, sources checked | One misquotation costs the credibility of the whole piece; one checked citation beats ten unchecked |
| Metaphor | It must fit the one point it is for. If it does not, cut it | A metaphor that does not fit is the image the reader remembers - the wrong one |
| Exaggeration | You may enlarge the degree. You may not change the relation between facts | Enlargement is rhetoric; changing the relation is fabrication |
| Reading badly | Fix it, do not settle | If it does not sound right, the sentence is wrong; that is not the reader's problem |
| Describing a place or an object | Follow the thing, and use few words | The thing is already elaborate; elaborate the words too and it drowns |
| Before handing it over | Five checks: forms of address and status used wrongly; a person compared to an object; a word used in only half its sense; borrowed material left unattributed; a source cited incorrectly | Clever lines get caught; clumsy mistakes cannot hide. These five are the ones most often missed |
| Reviewing someone else | Put down three biases first: preferring the old to the new, raising yourself by lowering them, taking the fake for the real. Read more than one piece before judging | If the bias arrives before the draft, what you are reviewing is not the draft |
| The six looks | One: does the form fit the occasion. Two: are the words used correctly. Three: is there anything new on the inherited form. Four: what is the ratio of sound to striking. Five: were the facts checked. Six: does it read well aloud | The order matters: if the first two fail, the other four are not worth doing |
| Judging a writer | Judge the work, not the position. Separate talent from effort | Adding a point for hardship, or subtracting one for prominence, is not reviewing |
| Judging a period | Look at the conditions of that period first | Writing follows its age; without the age you cannot read the change |

## What changes in Chinese

- **Characters, not words, are the unit.** Do not use rare characters: if one reader in three cannot read it, it is a bad character regardless of how right it is.
- **Do not stack characters sharing a radical.** A run of them turns the line into a texture instead of a sentence.
- **A character that recurs needs a reason.** Chinese has no plural inflection to hide behind; repetition is visible.
- **Alternate one-syllable and two-syllable words**, and settle it by reading aloud. This is where Chinese rhythm actually lives.
- **Antithesis and parallelism**: opposed meanings beat synonymous ones; alternate odd and even phrasing rather than running a full column of parallels, which turns into chanting and kills the rhythm.
- **Punctuation in a title**: delete the full stop, delete commas a space or a line break can carry.
- **No italics for emphasis.** There is no italic form for Chinese characters; software slants them geometrically, which is both ugly and harder to read. Use weight, grey level, indent, or size.

## What changes in English

- **Prefer the plain word to the Latinate one**, unless the Latinate one is genuinely more precise. *Use*, not *utilise*. *Start*, not *commence*.
- **Prefer the active voice**, unless the actor is genuinely unknown or genuinely irrelevant. The passive is where responsibility goes to hide.
- **Adverbs are usually a weak verb asking for help.** *Ran quickly* is *sprinted*. Cut the adverb, or change the verb.
- **Vary sentence length deliberately.** Three long sentences in a row is a drone; three short ones is a telegram. The variation is the rhythm.
- **Cut the throat-clearing.** *It is important to note that*, *in order to*, *the fact that*: the sentence usually starts one clause later than you wrote it.
- **Beware the achievement register** - *comprehensive*, *robust*, *seamless*, *leverage*, *state-of-the-art*. The swap test: could a competitor paste this sentence onto their own page unchanged? Then it says nothing.
- **When the rules make the sentence brutal, break them.** Plain is not the ceiling; a sentence that carries weight is allowed to be long, or fragmentary, or repetitive, if that is what carries the weight. Test: delete the word, the construction or the rhythm - if the meaning is unchanged but the sentence collapses, keep it.

## For a model working on someone's draft

1. **Do not decide for the writer.** Every item above has to be applied while looking at this particular draft; if you cannot see it in the draft, say you cannot see it rather than producing a suggestion because a suggestion is expected.
2. **Do not touch what the author wrote by hand.** List the places that break a rule, offer replacements, and change nothing until they say so. Facts, numbers and proper names are never changed.
3. **List the edits before handing back.** "Cut these four modifiers, replaced these three long words, this one sentence does not sound right." If you cannot list them, you did not revise the draft - you rewrote it.

## Boundaries

- Judgement and selection - is there enough material, what stays, what gets cut, what the piece is even about - happens before the first sentence, and is not in here.
- Typesetting and layout are not in here.
- This does not rank writers and does not hand out scores. It finds the specific thing that is wrong and says what to do about it.
